# Distinguish statements, decisions, and commitments

## The engineering or business decision

Meeting summarization becomes useful when it preserves who committed to what, by when, and with what dependencies. A suggestion is not a decision, and a mentioned person is not necessarily an owner. Capture unknown ownership explicitly. Use evidence spans so participants can challenge a proposed action without rereading the entire transcript.

The project uses invented meeting notes. In a real setting, recording consent, organizational policy, access control, and retention would shape the design. This workshop does not assume permission to record anyone or upload a real transcript. Build the workflow using the minimum data needed to test the concept.

## Worked example

“Maybe Sam could review the draft” is a suggestion. “I will review it by Thursday — Sam” is a commitment, but the date still needs a meeting reference date. A useful draft distinguishes `proposed_owner` from `confirmed_owner` and relative from resolved dates.

## Build it yourself

1. Write three fictional transcripts with decisions, proposals, reassignment, and unresolved dates.
2. Define a record with action, owner state, due-date state, dependency, and evidence span.
3. Annotate the transcripts manually and identify ambiguous commitments.
4. Design a confirmation step where a participant approves or corrects the extracted draft.

## Break it deliberately

A participant rejects a task later in the meeting. Your extraction must not retain the earlier assignment as confirmed.

## Acceptance checks

- Suggestions cannot silently become confirmed commitments.
- Every action has evidence or is explicitly unresolved.
- Relative dates are tied to a recorded reference date.
