# Choose a workflow and a buyer

## Understand the decision: Choose a workflow and a buyer

## Before you begin

Start with one working draft/review workflow. Use the fictional training-provider engagement; no real client or outreach is required.

## Why this decision matters

A service offer connects a specific buyer to a recurring problem you can credibly address. “I build AI agents” describes a method, not a customer outcome. Choose a workflow you understand well enough to observe its inputs, handoffs, exceptions, and cost of mistakes. Identify who experiences the pain, who approves the work, and who will use the result; these may be three different people.

Start with a low-risk read-only or draft workflow. Do not promise autonomous decisions, regulated advice, or operational savings before measuring the process. Your first goal is to establish whether the problem is frequent, costly enough to matter, and accessible with permissioned data. A simulated client is sufficient for the program exercises.

## Vocabulary used in this session

### Buyer

The person or role authorized to approve the commercial engagement.

### End user

The person who performs or benefits from the workflow in daily use.

### Problem hypothesis

An unverified claim about recurring pain that discovery must investigate.

## Learn each concept step by step

Work through these explanations before the completed example or exercise. Follow the inputs, mechanism and decision boundary rather than memorizing the definitions.

## Step 1 · Buyer

A buyer owns the decision to fund or authorize a workflow change. The person feeling the pain may lack budget authority. Map the operations lead’s decision, the evidence they need and unresolved authority before writing an offer. A role title is a hypothesis about responsibility, not proof that someone can approve the pilot.

## Step 2 · End user

An end user performs the workflow and experiences its corrections, waiting and exceptions. Here the assistant prepares a draft while the instructor reviews teaching facts. Saving assistant time can increase instructor effort. Observe both roles and ensure review capacity exists; otherwise faster drafting merely moves the bottleneck.

## Step 3 · Problem hypothesis

A problem hypothesis is a testable claim about frequency, effort, consequence and willingness to change. Forty monthly handouts is explicitly unverified in the specimen. Choose a discovery question that could disconfirm the opportunity. If volume falls or review capacity disappears, revisit the workflow instead of forcing an AI service into it.

## Buyer, workflow, and economic unit

### Start with the unit of work
A service becomes specific when it names one repeatable unit: a support ticket, a purchase record, or a participant handout. Write its trigger, input, current operator, output, and completion rule. A department is too large a unit for a first pilot. A single generated sentence is too small if it cannot finish an actual handoff. Use the fictional training provider throughout this course: the unit is one reviewed handout made from approved course notes.

### Separate three customers
The operator feels the friction, the operational owner is accountable for quality, and the budget owner can fund a change. An instructor may like your demo while the operations lead cannot provide source access and finance has not agreed to recurring costs. Draw three columns and put a named role in each. A single person may occupy multiple columns, but never infer authority from enthusiasm. Ask who accepts the deliverable and who maintains it after you leave.

### Score feasibility without hiding uncertainty
Use four gates before scoring attractiveness: permission to use representative inputs, an observable output standard, a reviewer with time, and a reversible fallback. Failure of a gate means investigate or decline. For opportunities that pass, compare monthly volume, minutes of avoidable work, cost of a wrong accepted output, and your familiarity with the workflow. Keep evidence and assumptions in separate fields. A score of four based on an assumption is not stronger evidence than a score of two based on observation.

### Worked opportunity comparison
Suppose course handouts occur 40 times a month, take 20 minutes each, and always receive instructor review. A payroll adjustment occurs 10 times, takes 8 minutes, and can move money. Even if payroll looks easier to automate technically, the first engagement has a more reversible draft boundary. Choose the handout workflow because you can observe review quality and stop before distribution. The decision is about the test you can run, not the most impressive demo.

### Write a falsifiable offer hypothesis
Use: “For [operator], we will test whether [bounded workflow] improves [observable measure], while [guardrail] stays within [limit], using [permitted inputs].” Add what would disprove it. For the training provider, a failed hypothesis might be that source notes are too incomplete for a reviewer to accept drafts without rewriting. That result should trigger source cleanup or a different service, rather than a stronger prompt.

### Practice record

Select one and explain which missing fact could reverse your decision. Your output is a defensible opportunity choice; it is not evidence that someone will buy it.

## Apply the three engineering pillars

## Software-first practice

Build interfaces, asynchronous services, API adapters and recoverable data flows before studying algorithm derivations. Use mathematics to explain a measured engineering decision.

## Production evidence

A notebook or chat demo is an early prototype. Completion requires operational evidence: deployment/readiness, bounded work, rate limits, error recovery and persisted state. The 10% framing is a teaching heuristic, not a measured universal percentage.

## Apply the three pillars to a discovery interview and opportunity scorecard

### Latency vs. Cost vs. Quality

Estimate response time, reviewer effort and cost of the current workflow; mark interview assumptions separately from observations. Compare at least two opportunities against a minimum accepted-output standard.

### Deterministic Evaluation

Turn interview claims into falsifiable workflow checks: input availability, reviewer authority and a repeated task. Reject an opportunity without usable evidence.

### Context Management

Inventory which approved source notes the workflow actually needs; exclude unrelated personal/client data and record source ownership.

## Failure and deployment work

Assign rate/budget caps, failure/manual fallback, hosting ownership, review duties, restore evidence and escalation before expanding the service.

## Evaluation precision

Automate repeatable checks with fixed inputs and versioned expectations. Model judges, faithfulness scores and semantic reviews can be stochastic; record judge/configuration versions and disagreement rather than calling them deterministic truth.
