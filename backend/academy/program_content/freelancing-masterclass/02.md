## Understand the decision: Conduct discovery with evidence

## Before you begin

## Why this decision matters

Discovery is an investigation of how work actually happens. Ask for a recent concrete example, the steps performed, exception cases, review effort, and consequences of a mistake. Avoid leading questions that only confirm enthusiasm for AI. Capture what was observed separately from the client’s interpretation and your own assumptions.

If no real client is available, use the supplied fictional engagement scenario and role-play the interview. Do not present invented interview results as customer validation. A useful discovery record includes unanswered questions and a reason the project might not be suitable. Permission to discuss a workflow is not permission to copy or upload its documents.

## Vocabulary used in this session

### Observation

Something directly seen or heard in the actual discovery exercise.

### Interpretation

Your explanation of what an observation might mean.

### Disconfirming evidence

An observation that would weaken the proposed problem or solution hypothesis.

## Learn each concept step by step

Work through these explanations before the completed example or exercise. Follow the inputs, mechanism and decision boundary rather than memorizing the definitions.

## Step 1 · Observation

An observation records what was actually seen or said, including source, date, sample and permission. A participant saying “daily” is an observed statement, not an observed daily count. A role-play must remain labeled simulated. Keep the raw statement alongside the sampled files so disagreement stays inspectable.

## Step 2 · Interpretation

An interpretation explains what the observation might mean. Two files could imply low volume or an incomplete folder. Do not average those possibilities into a fabricated monthly estimate. Ask about the unit of work and sample coverage before using numbers in a benefit calculation or commercial promise.

## Step 3 · Disconfirming evidence

Disconfirming evidence would weaken the proposed service: no recurring task, no authorized inputs, no reviewer or corrections costing more than manual preparation. Ask for a recent exception and invite a reason not to automate. Leading questions such as “Would AI save you time?” collect approval language rather than operational evidence.

## Discovery interviews and evidence synthesis

### Observe the work before proposing a solution
A discovery interview should reconstruct a recent completed case. Start with “Show me the last handout you prepared.” Ask which source arrived, how the operator decided what to include, which correction took longest, who approved it, and what happened after approval. Follow the artifact through the workflow. Asking whether AI would help invites a prediction; observing a recent correction gives you evidence.

### Use an interview sequence
Begin with context and permission. Move to a specific recent example. Trace its normal path. Ask for an exception and its consequence. Identify the people and systems involved. Finish by summarizing your understanding and asking what you missed. Keep questions neutral: “What happens when a note is incomplete?” gives more information than “Would automatic completion solve missing notes?” The latter hides the possibility that completing missing information is unacceptable.

### Keep an evidence ledger
Use four columns: observation, interpretation, assumption, and unresolved question. “The instructor corrected three objectives in the example” is an observation. “Objectives are the largest source of rework” is an interpretation requiring more examples. “This happens every week” is an assumption until you inspect records or hear a supported account. Write a source and date beside each observation. Mark role-play data as simulated.

### Resolve disagreement instead of averaging it
An operator says review takes ten minutes; a manager says two. Both can be describing different intervals. Ask each to identify start and end events. One may count source lookup while the other measures only approval clicks. Define a common timing rule, observe a case, and keep the disagreement in the record until resolved. Do not average incompatible measurements into a confident six-minute baseline.

### Handle access as a separate decision
Permission to discuss a document is not permission to upload it. Use a redacted or synthetic sample during initial discovery. Record who can authorize production access, which fields can leave the organization, and whether provider processing is permitted. If those answers are missing, your next deliverable may be a data-access map rather than a prototype.

### Interview exercise
Role-play an operator who wants faster drafts and an instructor whose main problem is factual errors. Ask eight questions without proposing a product. Produce a current-state map, three observations, two contradictions, one disconfirming finding, and a follow-up test. A good discovery summary can recommend no build when the bottleneck is approval ownership rather than text generation.

## Apply the three engineering pillars

## Software-first practice

Build interfaces, asynchronous services, API adapters and recoverable data flows before studying algorithm derivations. Use mathematics to explain a measured engineering decision.

## Production evidence

A notebook or chat demo is an early prototype. Graduation builds toward operational evidence: deployment/readiness, bounded work, rate limits, error recovery and persisted state. Early checkpoints assess only boundaries already taught; future deployment controls are planned rather than claimed complete. The 10% framing is a teaching heuristic, not a measured universal percentage.

## Apply the three pillars to a discovery interview and opportunity scorecard

### Latency vs. Cost vs. Quality

Estimate response time, reviewer effort and cost of the current workflow; mark interview assumptions separately from observations. Compare at least two opportunities against a minimum accepted-output standard.

### Deterministic Evaluation

Turn interview claims into falsifiable workflow checks: input availability, reviewer authority and a repeated task. Reject an opportunity without usable evidence.

### Context Management

Inventory which approved source notes the workflow actually needs; exclude unrelated personal/client data and record source ownership.

## Failure and deployment work

Assign rate/budget caps, failure/manual fallback, hosting ownership, review duties, restore evidence and escalation before expanding the service.

## Evaluation precision

Automate repeatable checks with fixed inputs and versioned expectations. Model judges, faithfulness scores and semantic reviews can be stochastic; record judge/configuration versions and disagreement rather than calling them deterministic truth.
