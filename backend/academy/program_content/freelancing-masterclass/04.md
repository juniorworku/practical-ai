# Package a service with a bounded outcome

## Understand the decision: Package a service with a bounded outcome

## Before you begin

Bring your artifacts from sessions 1–3 of this program. Continue the same fictional training-provider engagement and keep assumption labels and revision IDs intact.

## Why this decision matters

A well-scoped service describes deliverables, supported inputs, exclusions, dependencies, and acceptance. It should be understandable without knowing which model or framework you use. Package a diagnostic, a pilot, and a possible expansion separately so the client can make a decision after seeing evidence.

Your offer must align with what you can support. If you have only tested plain English text, do not include arbitrary PDFs, scans, voice notes, and multilingual documents under “document automation.” Make the reviewer’s role and the manual fallback visible. A bounded promise is more credible and easier to price than an open-ended commitment to improve everything.

## Vocabulary used in this session

### Service package

A bounded outcome with supported inputs, deliverables, exclusions and review duties.

### Exclusion

A capability or effect that is explicitly outside the engagement.

### Expansion gate

Evidence and a separate decision required before broadening scope.

## Service packaging and staged commitments

### Package a decision, not an unlimited capability
A diagnostic, a pilot, and production support answer different questions. A diagnostic identifies the workflow and feasibility. A pilot tests a bounded change with named users and representative inputs. Production requires ownership, access controls, monitoring, support, and recovery. Give each stage its own deliverable and decision gate. A successful diagnostic does not silently authorize production use.

### Build a service boundary
Describe supported input formats, volume, roles, output, review duty, and exclusions. For example, the handout pilot accepts approved UTF-8 course notes in one template, produces draft handouts with source IDs, and requires instructor approval. It excludes scanned documents, new curriculum creation, automated distribution, and confidential learner data. Exclusions are useful when they prevent a buyer from inferring a capability you have not tested.

### Make dependencies visible
List what the client must provide: representative samples, a reviewer, access decisions, a test environment, and an acceptance owner. Attach a date and consequence to each dependency. If approved samples arrive late, the evaluation schedule changes. Avoid a fixed delivery date that assumes inputs you do not control. A dependency table makes negotiation about the actual work possible.

### Offer useful alternatives
If the source material is inconsistent, offer a data-readiness diagnostic before implementation. If the buyer cannot assign a reviewer, offer a read-only search tool rather than a drafting workflow that needs review. Alternatives should preserve the desired outcome while changing the operational burden. They should not be arbitrary tiers that differ only in the number of prompts.

### Test the offer with teach-back
Give the one-page service description to a reviewer and ask: What will you receive? What can it do with incomplete notes? Who approves a draft? What costs recur? What happens when it fails? Any mismatch between their answers and your intent is a specification defect. Revise the offer before treating agreement as informed.

### Packaging exercise
Write diagnostic, pilot, and expansion cards. Each needs a decision question, inputs, outputs, exclusions, client duties, acceptance evidence, and stop condition. Use fictional costs if practicing. No card should guarantee an income, accuracy rate, or saving that has not been measured.

## A bounded pilot offer card

## Completed specimen: A bounded pilot offer card

**Decision to support:** can instructors prepare source-linked handout drafts for one approved text-note template with manageable review?

**Inputs:** up to 20 synthetic English UTF-8 notes, one template, one instructor reviewer. **Output:** editable draft with source references, missing-objective flags and local reviewed export. **Deliverables:** runnable prototype, case-level evaluation, cost assumptions and handover guide.

**Excluded:** scanned PDF/OCR, new curriculum invention, automatic email, public publication, learner records, multilingual output. **Client duties in the simulation:** supply template and reviewer time; name an acceptance owner. **Fallback:** existing manual preparation remains available.

**Gate:** diagnostic first establishes fields and baseline; pilot tests the agreed cases; expansion is a new decision based on results. The offer does not guarantee time savings, income or autonomous accuracy. A teach-back that interprets “export” as “send to students” means the offer needs revision before agreement.

## Critique and transfer

Create diagnostic and expansion cards with different decision questions. Have a peer teach back the pilot deliverables and exclusions; record and correct any misunderstanding.

Treat the completed specimen as a model of reasoning, not a result to copy into your own evidence. Your submission must replace its assumptions and statuses with your own observations, or remain explicitly simulated.

## Build and challenge your own version

## Worked example

Pilot: “Draft participant handouts from one approved course template, with source references and instructor review.” Inputs: up to 20 synthetic text notes for testing. Exclusions: public publishing, automatic email, scanned PDFs, and changes to approved curriculum. Deliverables: prototype, evaluation report, and handover instructions.

## Build it yourself

1. Write a one-page service offer for the simulated client.
2. Define supported input formats, volume, users, review step, and output.
3. List at least five exclusions that prevent ambiguous expectations.
4. Describe the evidence required before offering an expanded engagement.

## Break it deliberately

The buyer assumes the pilot includes writing new curriculum and sending it to students. Revise the offer so those effects cannot be mistaken for included work.

## Acceptance checks

- The offer describes an outcome and tangible artifacts.
- Input boundaries and review duties are explicit.
- Expansion requires a separate decision.
