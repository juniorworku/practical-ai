# Estimate effort, support, and pricing assumptions

## Understand the decision: Estimate effort, support, and pricing assumptions

## Before you begin

## Why this decision matters

An estimate should account for discovery, implementation, testing, review, deployment, handover, and support. Separate one-time work from recurring costs. Include uncertainty and the assumptions that could change the estimate. A price is a commercial decision; a cost model helps you understand what the engagement requires before making a promise.

Use fictional currency units in this exercise so the reasoning is independent of market rates. Model API usage from expected volume and observed calls, not only one successful response. Include retries, failed runs, review effort, and maintenance. Do not present a speculative return on investment as a guaranteed saving.

## Vocabulary used in this session

### One-time effort

Work such as discovery, implementation, testing and initial handover.

### Recurring cost

Ongoing usage, hosting, review or support effort after the initial build.

### Contingency

Explicit planning allowance for uncertainty rather than hidden guaranteed profit.

## Learn each concept step by step

Work through these explanations before the completed example or exercise. Follow the inputs, mechanism and decision boundary rather than memorizing the definitions.

## Step 1 · One-time effort

One-time effort includes discovery, build, evaluation, review and handover. Break it into packages with assumptions and high-case drivers instead of estimating only coding. Forty-four hours is an estimate, not a time log. A contingency allowance is additional planning capacity; do not quietly use it to promise more scope.

## Step 2 · Recurring cost

Recurring cost includes hosting, all provider attempts, review, maintenance and incident work. Usage cost per accepted draft divides all attempt spend by reviewed accepted outputs. It excludes reviewer time unless explicitly included. Hosting and support do not disappear because the implementation fee was paid once.

## Step 3 · Contingency

Contingency represents uncertainty under named assumptions. Low/high estimates reveal which dependency drives the range. Capacity must also account for existing support: fifteen support hours cannot simultaneously be sold as implementation time. Keep internal labor cost, commercial price and buyer value separate; fictional rates are not market recommendations.

## Effort estimation, unit economics, and support capacity

### Estimate by work package
Break the work into discovery, data preparation, implementation, evaluation, deployment, review, training, and handover. Give each a low, expected, and high effort estimate with the source of uncertainty. “Build app: 40 hours” hides which assumption can change the schedule. A dependency on an unknown document format should appear as a risk or investigation task, not an invisible buffer.

### Separate cost, price, and value
Cost is what delivery consumes. Price is the commercial amount agreed. Value is the outcome the buyer cares about. These quantities are related but not interchangeable. For an illustrative 44-hour pilot at 50 fictional units per hour, direct labor cost is 2,200 units; a 20% effort allowance gives 52.8 hours, or 2,640 units. Hosting, provider usage, and support are separate. These are teaching assumptions, not recommended market rates.

### Model recurring usage from accepted work
Count requests, calls per request, retries, input/output tokens, and failed jobs. Divide total cost by accepted outputs rather than only successful API calls. If 100 attempts produce 70 accepted handouts, your unit denominator is 70. Include a review cost and maintenance reserve. A model that is cheap per call can be expensive per accepted result if it generates more corrections.

### Price a boundary you can explain
Fixed-scope pricing needs clear acceptance and change rules. Time-based work needs reporting and a budget control. Ongoing support needs a coverage window, supported versions, and severity definitions. Do not make a fixed promise against an unbounded input set. Choose a model for the simulated engagement and explain which risks each party retains.

### Check your own capacity
Suppose three supported clients each generate four incidents per month, with 45 minutes of investigation and 30 minutes of follow-up per incident. That is 15 support hours before planned maintenance. Add sales and administration when evaluating how much delivery work you can take on. Revenue alone does not establish a sustainable service.

## Apply the three engineering pillars

## Software-first practice

Build interfaces, asynchronous services, API adapters and recoverable data flows before studying algorithm derivations. Use mathematics to explain a measured engineering decision.

## Production evidence

A notebook or chat demo is an early prototype. Completion requires operational evidence: deployment/readiness, bounded work, rate limits, error recovery and persisted state. The 10% framing is a teaching heuristic, not a measured universal percentage.

## Apply the three pillars to a fixed-scope statement of work

### Latency vs. Cost vs. Quality

Specify latency/quality limits and an explicit usage allowance in the offer. Price review, retries and maintenance, not just a successful generation.

### Deterministic Evaluation

Write Given/When/Then acceptance examples and critical exclusions into the scope. Link each promise to an observable test and a named decision owner.

### Context Management

Define allowed sources, revisions, retention and context allowance; a source expansion or new tenant changes scope and cache handling.

## Failure and deployment work

Assign rate/budget caps, failure/manual fallback, hosting ownership, review duties, restore evidence and escalation before expanding the service.

## Evaluation precision

Automate repeatable checks with fixed inputs and versioned expectations. Model judges, faithfulness scores and semantic reviews can be stochastic; record judge/configuration versions and disagreement rather than calling them deterministic truth.
