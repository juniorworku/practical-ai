## Understand the decision: Train users and run acceptance testing

## Before you begin

## Why this decision matters

Training should teach the workflow, review responsibilities, and failure response rather than only where to click. Users need to know when to trust a draft, how to inspect evidence, when to escalate, and how to recover from an unavailable service. Give them realistic cases they have not seen before.

Acceptance testing belongs to the authorized reviewer using the agreed scenarios. Record passed, failed, deferred, and not-tested separately. A user attending a demo does not establish acceptance. A useful handover rehearsal observes someone else operating the system without the implementer correcting every step.

## Vocabulary used in this session

### Acceptance session

An authorized reviewer checks agreed scenarios and records their actual outcomes.

### Not tested

No observation establishes whether a condition passes or fails.

### Handover rehearsal

Another person uses the guide and system without continual author intervention.

## Learn each concept step by step

Work through these explanations before the completed example or exercise. Follow the inputs, mechanism and decision boundary rather than memorizing the definitions.

## Step 1 · Acceptance session

An acceptance session asks an authorized user to perform defined tasks on a candidate revision. Demonstrating a feature for them measures attendance, not independent use. Include missing information and failure cases, preserve any assistance you give and ask the trainee to explain why a draft cannot yet be distributed.

## Step 2 · Not tested

Not tested is a distinct status from passed, failed and out of scope. A missing instructor makes factual acceptance untested; an optional PDF outside the agreed input boundary is deferred. Mixing those categories produces false green reports. Keep failed first attempts after a correction so the reason for changed teaching remains visible.

## Step 3 · Handover rehearsal

A handover rehearsal checks whether instructions and system labels support independent operation. “Draft complete” can mislead a trainee into bypassing review. Change the label and instructions, then observe a fresh input without coaching. Repeating the same coached case to success does not establish the new instruction works independently.

## Training design and user acceptance rehearsal

### Teach decisions before clicks
Users need to know what is a draft, how to inspect source evidence, what makes an output unacceptable, and when to escalate. A click tour on a perfect example does not teach those decisions. Design training around a normal case, an incomplete source, a contradiction, an unavailable provider, and a stale edit.

### Define role-specific tasks
An operator creates a draft; a reviewer accepts or rejects a revision; an administrator manages access. Teach each role its own responsibility and boundary. Do not imply that a green generation status means approval. Give the reviewer a short rubric and show how corrections affect the saved artifact.

### Observe teach-back
Ask the trainee to complete an unseen case and explain why they accepted or rejected it. Avoid taking over the mouse or supplying the answer at every hesitation. Record confusion, source lookups, mistakes, and time. Those observations evaluate both the guide and the interface.

### Run acceptance separately from training
Acceptance belongs to the authorized owner using agreed scenarios and evidence. Attending training is not acceptance. Keep passed, failed, deferred, and not tested distinct. A failed recovery scenario cannot be hidden behind a successful generation demo. Update the acceptance record when a defect is repaired and retested.

### Build a usable quick-start guide
Include purpose, supported inputs, normal steps, review rubric, common failure symptoms, fallback procedure, and escalation owner. Use synthetic examples. Include version and review date so a changed interface does not leave users following stale instructions.

### Training exercise
Write a five-case rehearsal. Have another person or a clearly labeled role-play operator use your guide. Capture the observed result and one improvement per failure. End with an acceptance record that identifies unresolved conditions and who can decide whether they block handover.

## Apply the three engineering pillars

## Software-first practice

Build interfaces, asynchronous services, API adapters and recoverable data flows before studying algorithm derivations. Use mathematics to explain a measured engineering decision.

## Production evidence

A notebook or chat demo is an early prototype. Graduation builds toward operational evidence: deployment/readiness, bounded work, rate limits, error recovery and persisted state. Early checkpoints assess only boundaries already taught; future deployment controls are planned rather than claimed complete. The 10% framing is a teaching heuristic, not a measured universal percentage.

## Apply the three pillars to an operator runbook and incident drill

### Latency vs. Cost vs. Quality

Include routine usage, alert response, fallback and restore labor in the ongoing service budget. Compare capacity assumptions with actual drill observations.

### Deterministic Evaluation

Have a fresh operator restart, read back, deny wrong-owner access and restore isolated synthetic records; attach transcripts and unresolved failures.

### Context Management

Define who can refresh data, revoke access, invalidate caches and prune logs. A handover needs lifecycle ownership, not a folder of prompts.

## Failure and deployment work

Assign rate/budget caps, failure/manual fallback, hosting ownership, review duties, restore evidence and escalation before expanding the service.

## Evaluation precision

Automate repeatable checks with fixed inputs and versioned expectations. Model judges, faithfulness scores and semantic reviews can be stochastic; record judge/configuration versions and disagreement rather than calling them deterministic truth.
