# Write an evidence-based case study

## Understand the decision: Write an evidence-based case study

## Before you begin

Bring your artifacts from sessions 1–15 of this program. Continue the same fictional training-provider engagement and keep assumption labels and revision IDs intact.

## Why this decision matters

A credible case study explains the client context, constraints, decisions, implementation, observed results, and remaining limitations. Ask for permission before using real client names, logos, quotes, or data. A self-directed project is valid when labeled accurately. Do not invent testimonials or imply a simulated buyer is a paying customer.

Use the same baseline and evaluation definitions from discovery. If the pilot improved one measure but worsened another, include the trade-off. Screenshots can illustrate a workflow, but they do not replace evidence of persistence, access control, or factual support. Show the artifact that substantiates each technical claim.

## Vocabulary used in this session

### Permissioned case study

A portfolio uses client identity or data only with appropriate permission.

### Simulated engagement

A self-directed scenario accurately labeled rather than represented as a paying client.

### Claim-evidence map

Each result statement points to its baseline, evaluation or implementation artifact.

## Portfolio claims and evidence-backed case studies

### Make one claim per evidence trail
A case study should connect context, constraint, decision, implementation, measured result, and limitation. For each result, identify the dataset, baseline, metric, sample size, and artifact. “Built an AI assistant” is a capability description. “Saved drafts persisted after restart on the local test stack” is a scoped technical observation.

### Label simulated work accurately
A self-directed project can demonstrate useful engineering. Call the training provider fictional and the engagement simulated. Do not turn role-play feedback into a testimonial or say a paying client used the service. Accuracy about provenance makes the technical evidence easier to trust.

### Show a consequential failure
Include an example that changed your design: a missing objective produced invented text, a stale edit overwrote approval, or an export included an unreviewed draft. Explain how you reproduced the failure, what changed, and which test now catches it. A polished screenshot without this reasoning does not show how you handle delivery risk.

### Separate local results from general claims
A ten-document synthetic evaluation can support a statement about those cases and settings. It cannot establish performance on every client document or predict business savings. Show the remaining uncertainty and the next validation step. Avoid a percentage without a denominator or a comparison without a common rubric.

### Protect others' information
Use synthetic artifacts or explicit permission for names, data, quotes, and logos. Redact credentials, internal URLs, and private records from logs and images. A public repository is not automatically a suitable place for a client's operational details.

### Case-study exercise
Write a one-page narrative and a claim-to-evidence table. Include one architecture sketch, a workflow example, a before/after comparison, an important failure, and an operational limitation. Ask a peer to challenge your strongest claim and narrow it until every word is supported.

## A case-study claim audit

## Completed specimen: A case-study claim audit

| Draft claim | Supporting evidence required | Defensible wording |
|---|---|---|
| “Saved a client hours” | actual client baseline and comparable timed work | “Simulated engagement; ten synthetic notes” until measured |
| “Review fell from 8 to 6 minutes” | same task definition, case-level times and sample | “In a ten-note synthetic comparison, mean review was 8 versus 6 minutes” if actually observed |
| “Secure app” | much broader evidence than this pilot | “Cross-account read denied in the recorded ownership cases” |
| “Production-ready” | operations, load, recovery and organizational acceptance | “Offline reviewed prototype with stated limits” |

If the two-minute difference is observed over ten notes, that is 20 minutes on that sample, not proof of future monthly savings. Include differences in errors or reviewer workload. Describe one failed attempt, its cause and the test that motivated the correction.

No customer quote, logo or simulated buyer is presented as a real endorsement. Specific evidence preserves the value of a self-directed project without inventing a commercial history.

## Critique and transfer

Audit five statements in your own case study. Link each to a real evidence file or narrow/remove it. Include a failure and clearly label every simulated result.

Treat the completed specimen as a model of reasoning, not a result to copy into your own evidence. Your submission must replace its assumptions and statuses with your own observations, or remain explicitly simulated.

## Build and challenge your own version

## Worked example

“Simulated engagement for a training provider: draft review time fell from eight to six minutes on ten synthetic notes, while two outputs required source clarification.” This is a scoped observation. It does not establish market demand or predict a client’s future savings.

## Build it yourself

1. Write a case study using only synthetic or permissioned artifacts.
2. Map each result claim to a baseline, evaluation record, or test.
3. Include one failed attempt and the decision it changed.
4. Review all labels for simulated versus actual work.

## Break it deliberately

A portfolio draft says “helped a client” when the engagement was fictional. Correct it and preserve the value through specific implementation evidence.

## Acceptance checks

- No invented customer, result, or testimonial appears.
- Claims include sample size and scope.
- A failure and its correction are explained.
