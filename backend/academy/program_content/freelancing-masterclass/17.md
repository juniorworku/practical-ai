# Prepare outreach and qualification responsibly

## Qualification and problem-specific outreach preparation

### Research a plausible workflow, then ask
Published information can suggest an operating context; it rarely proves an internal problem. A business running many courses may prepare handouts, but you cannot infer that its current process is broken. Write the public fact, your inference, and the question you would ask in separate fields.

### Qualify fit before proposing delivery
Check problem relevance, owner authority, access feasibility, review capacity, timing, and the ability to measure an outcome. A buyer who wants fully autonomous publication but cannot assign a reviewer may not fit your current service. A respectful decline or a diagnostic is preferable to an unsupported promise.

### Draft a concise introduction
Use a relevant public observation, a specific workflow hypothesis, one accurately labeled piece of evidence, and an invitation to discuss the current process. Do not invent urgency, savings, or a relationship. For the exercise, use fictional organizations and keep messages as local drafts.

### Prepare useful follow-up questions
Ask for a recent example, the current completion rule, what causes rework, who reviews results, and which data can be used in a pilot. Avoid requesting confidential files before access is agreed. If someone declines, record the outcome and do not turn follow-up into pressure.

### Evaluate your own readiness
Before offering a capability, confirm you can demonstrate its normal behavior, a failure, evidence of persistence, the review boundary, and a recovery path. A demo built on hand-picked examples may be suitable for learning but insufficient for a real engagement. Qualify yourself as carefully as the prospect.

### Qualification exercise
Create three fictional prospect records, separate observations from assumptions, draft an introduction, and write five discovery questions. Define three decline conditions. Have a peer role-play an enthusiastic but unsuitable buyer and explain a bounded alternative without promising results you have not observed.

## Define the Outcome Before You Choose the Output

## Output Is Not Outcome

“Build an incident assistant” names an output. It does not say who needs it, what becomes better, or what must remain safe.

An outcome frame says:

> When a production alert arrives, the on-call engineer identifies the failing service and a safe next action within two minutes, while diagnosis remains read-only and auditable.

That sentence can be satisfied by software, a runbook, a data repair, or a smaller interface change. It keeps the team attached to the result rather than the first artifact someone imagined.

## The Six-Part Frame

| Part | Question |
|---|---|
| User | Who experiences the problem directly? |
| Situation | When and where does it occur? |
| Current behavior | What happens today, including workarounds? |
| Desired outcome | What observable state should improve? |
| Constraints | Which safety, policy, cost, or compatibility limits are fixed? |
| Non-goals | What tempting adjacent work is excluded? |

**System flow**

- User and situation → Current behavior
- Desired outcome
- Constraints
- Non-goals
- Evidence question

## Find Solution Leakage

Outcome statements leak solutions when they contain a product form, interface, model choice, framework, or architecture that has not been earned by evidence.

- “Users receive a weekly AI summary” leaks the summary and cadence.
- “Users understand account changes before approval” states the result.
- “Deploy a vector database” leaks infrastructure.
- “Relevant policy evidence is available during review” states a capability.

Constraints can name technology when compatibility truly fixes it. Record why it is fixed.

## Constraints Protect the Outcome

Constraints are not implementation details. They are part of the real-world goal:

- no production writes during diagnosis;
- response within the incident time budget;
- existing audit events remain authoritative;
- no new runtime dependency;
- accessibility behavior remains intact.

A build that reaches the outcome by violating a constraint has not reached the outcome.

## Non-Goals Create a Boundary

Non-goals prevent a useful slice from turning into a platform. Good non-goals are concrete enough to reject work:

- no automatic remediation;
- no new alert-routing system;
- no replacement of the incident commander;
- no historical analytics in this slice.

## Discover the Workflow People Actually Perform

## Start with the Current System

Do not begin by asking what features people want. Begin by reconstructing what happens now.

For each step, record:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Actor | On-call engineer |
| Trigger | Production alert arrives |
| Action | Opens alert, then searches dashboards |
| Input | Alert payload and deployment record |
| Output | Candidate service and owner |
| Friction | Context switching across three tools |
| Authority | Incident commander approves a write |
| Evidence | Screen recording, incident log, runbook |

The workflow is larger than the screen. It includes waiting, copy-paste, side channels, approval, error recovery, and the steps people have stopped noticing.

## Evidence Has Strength

Use a simple evidence ladder:

1. **Direct behavior:** observation, trace, recording, or system event.
2. **Artifact:** ticket, runbook, log, form, or completed output.
3. **Reported behavior:** a person describes what they do.
4. **Inference:** the team concludes what probably happens.

All four can be useful. Only the first two prove current behavior directly. Label the rest so confidence does not silently inflate.

**System flow**

- Trigger → Actor action
- Handoff
- Next actor action
- Outcome
- Direct evidence
- Artifact
- Reported behavior

## Search for Four Things

- **Friction:** repeated effort, delay, re-entry, or recovery.
- **Hidden state:** facts carried in memory, chat, or personal notes.
- **Authority:** the person or system allowed to make a consequential change.
- **Exceptions:** the case where the normal workflow stops being normal.

AI features often fail at handoffs and exceptions because the happy path was the only path shaped.

## Do Not Average Away Disagreement

Two users can perform different workflows for good reasons. Preserve the variants until you understand whether they represent:

- different roles;
- different risk levels;
- legacy and current process;
- expertise differences;
- a genuine policy disagreement.

An averaged workflow can describe nobody.

## Discover the Workflow People Actually Perform · implementation walkthrough

Read the control flow before running or changing it. This pinned teaching implementation illustrates the chapter; source-specific dependencies and fixtures are named in its code. The short exercise in the Exercise tab is a separate standard-library practice.

```python
# Lesson program: audits workflow evidence, ordering, confidence, and friction.
# Lesson: phases/14-agent-engineering/48-discover-the-real-workflow/docs/en.md
# Canonical source: Nuseibeh and Easterbrook, Requirements Engineering: A Roadmap.
# Canonical source: Gotel and Finkelstein, ICRE 1994, DOI 10.1109/ICRE.1994.292398.
from __future__ import annotations

import json
from dataclasses import asdict, dataclass
from pathlib import Path


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Evidence:
    source: str
    observation: str
    direct: bool
    confidence: float


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class WorkflowStep:
    order: int
    actor: str
    action: str
    evidence: tuple[Evidence, ...]
    friction: str = ""


def audit(steps: list[WorkflowStep]) -> dict:
    orders = [step.order for step in steps]
    issues: list[str] = []
    if orders != list(range(1, len(steps) + 1)):
        issues.append("workflow order must be contiguous from one")
    for step in steps:
        if not step.evidence:
            issues.append(f"step {step.order} has no evidence")
        for item in step.evidence:
            if not 0 <= item.confidence <= 1:
                issues.append(f"step {step.order} has confidence outside zero to one")
    direct = sum(item.direct for step in steps for item in step.evidence)
    total = sum(len(step.evidence) for step in steps)
    return {
        "status": "grounded" if not issues and direct > 0 else "needs-evidence",
        "issues": issues,
        "direct_evidence_ratio": round(direct / total, 2) if total else 0,
        "friction_points": [step.friction for step in steps if step.friction],
        "steps": [asdict(step) for step in steps],
    }


def example() -> list[WorkflowStep]:
    return [
        WorkflowStep(1, "on-call engineer", "opens the alert", (Evidence("screen recording 01", "alert lacks service owner", True, 0.95),), "owner lookup"),
        WorkflowStep(2, "on-call engineer", "searches dashboards", (Evidence("incident 184", "three dashboards opened", True, 0.9),), "context switching"),
        WorkflowStep(3, "incident commander", "approves mitigation", (Evidence("runbook", "production writes require approval", False, 0.8),)),
    ]


def main() -> None:
    output = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1] / "outputs" / "workflow-evidence.json"
    output.write_text(json.dumps(audit(example()), indent=2) + "\n", encoding="utf-8")
    print(output.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

## Apply it: Prepare outreach and qualification responsibly

## The engineering or business decision

Outreach should connect a plausible workflow problem to relevant evidence, not promise generic AI transformation. Research an organization’s published business information and distinguish what you know from what you infer. A useful first conversation asks permission to explore a specific problem and makes it easy to decline.

This lesson prepares drafts only; it does not send messages or scrape private contacts. Use fictional prospects for practice or published business channels with the proper authorization in a real engagement. Qualification checks whether the buyer has a relevant problem, authority, feasible data access, timing, and a realistic review process. Declining a poor fit protects both sides.

## Worked example

A local draft says “Your public course page mentions several live cohorts. I built a synthetic handout-review prototype that may be relevant to preparation work. If that workflow is a priority, I’d be interested in understanding your current process.” It avoids claiming the organization has a problem you have not observed.

## Build it yourself

1. Create three fictional prospect profiles with evidence and assumptions separated.
2. Draft a short problem-specific introduction and a follow-up question for each.
3. Write a qualification checklist and three reasons to decline.
4. Prepare a discovery agenda that requests no sensitive documents before access is agreed.

## Break it deliberately

A prospect wants a fully autonomous workflow involving confidential records with no review. Explain the scope or evidence conditions needed before proceeding.

## Acceptance checks

- Drafts distinguish published facts from assumptions.
- No message is sent as part of the exercise.
- Qualification includes authority, data, and review feasibility.
